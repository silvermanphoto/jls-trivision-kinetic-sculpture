Why those pairings

The motor has two separate coils. Terminals 1A and 1B are the two ends of one coil; 2A and 2B are the two ends of the other. Wires from different coils must never share a pair.

Swapping a pair is harmless

If you reverse the two wires within one coil — black and green with each other, say — the motor still runs perfectly, it just turns the other way. Only mixing wires between the two coils causes a fault.

Verify by meter, not by colour

After a rewiring, an extension lead may have been swapped and the colours can lie. With the 24V off, measure resistance between wire ends:

BetweenShould readMeaning
Black and greenabout 1.45 Ωthese two are one coil
Red and blueabout 1.45 Ωthese two are the other coil
Black and redopen circuitdifferent coils, correctly isolated

Any pair reading near 1.45 ohms is a coil. Any pair reading open is across two different coils. The 1.45 ohm figure is the resistance-per-phase from the datasheet.

Why all twelve motors pulse on power-up

This is a power supply problem, not a wiring problem.

Three facts together settle it:

  1. The identification sketch now on the Arduino sends no step pulses at all until you type a command. Nothing is telling the motors to move.
  2. All twelve pulse together. A wiring mistake affects one motor. Twelve motors moving in unison share only one thing — the power rail.
  3. The Mean Well LRS-150-24 protects itself in hiccup mode: on overload it shuts down, waits, retries, and keeps retrying until the load drops. Every retry gives all twelve motors a jolt. That is the pulse.

The arithmetic

Each motor holding still at its full 2 amps per phase burns roughly 11.6 watts in the windings. Twelve of them is about 139 watts, and the drivers are not perfectly efficient, so the supply sees around 155 watts — close to 6.5 amps at 24 volts.

What to do, in order

  1. Prove it in two minutes. Unplug eleven motors at the terminal blocks and leave one connected. Power up. If the pulsing stops, the supply was the cause and the wiring is fine.
  2. Watch the rail. Put a meter across the 24V output while it pulses. If the voltage collapses and recovers in time with the pulsing, that is the hiccup, seen directly.
  3. Turn the current dials down. Each board's small potentiometer, marked ILimit 0 to 2A, is almost certainly near maximum. You do not need 2 amps. At three-quarters of one revolution per minute the prisms ask very little of the motors. Around 1 amp puts the whole sculpture near 35 watts instead of 155 — comfortable, cooler, and quieter.
  4. Bring them back one at a time. Reconnect motors in small groups, checking after each that the supply stays steady.
  5. Then run the identification sweep to sort out which channel drives which prism.
A note on the black-and-red theory

Swapping black and red would not produce this symptom. The two coils are electrically separate, so connecting one wire from each coil to the same bridge leaves that bridge with no circuit at all. A motor wired that way sits dead and silent — it does not buzz. Worth confirming with the meter regardless, but it is not what you are seeing.

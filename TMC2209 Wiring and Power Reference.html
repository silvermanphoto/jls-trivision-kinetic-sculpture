Why those pairings

The motor has two separate coils. Terminals 1A and 1B are the two ends of one coil; 2A and 2B are the two ends of the other. Wires from different coils must never share a pair.

Swapping a pair is harmless

If you reverse the two wires within one coil — black and green with each other, say — the motor still runs perfectly, it just turns the other way. Only mixing wires between the two coils causes a fault.

Two different green wires

Green on the pin header means direction. Green on the screw terminal means one end of motor coil A. They are unrelated and go to different connectors — worth keeping straight when tracing a cable.

Leaving the remaining pins unconnected — mostly right, one worth changing

Your manual records MS1, MS2, DIAG, INDEX, UART and EN as unconnected on every driver. Two of those are exactly right:

PinLeft unconnectedResult
ENfineDriver enabled. Adafruit's guide: "pull this pin high to disable the output to the motors," so floating leaves it running.
MS1, MS2fine1/8 microstepping, the board's default — 3200 microsteps per revolution, which is what the sketch's step maths assumes.
UART (header pin 9)worth changingThis is also the automatic standstill power-down control. Floating means the feature is not reliably on. Grounding it cuts resting power to under a third — see the section below.

So none of these is the source of the pulsing, but one of them is leaving a large power saving on the table.

Verify by meter, not by colour

After a rewiring, an extension lead may have been swapped and the colours can lie. With the 24V off, measure resistance between wire ends:

Any pair reading near 1.45 ohms is a coil. Any pair reading open is across two different coils. The 1.45 ohm figure is the resistance-per-phase from the datasheet.

Setting the current dial, and knowing the number in advance

Corrected 29 July 2026 after reading Adafruit's schematic for this exact board. The figures further down this page were wrong; they are fixed here.

The little dial sets a reference voltage that tells the chip how much current to push through the motor. You can read that voltage with a multimeter and set it deliberately instead of guessing.

Where to put the probes

The dial is a Bourns 3314J trimmer. Its body and its adjustment slot are plastic and connected to nothing — touching them reads zero no matter what else is right. The three tiny metal legs soldered to the board are the only electrical contacts.

Hold the board with the green screw terminal at the top and the row of pins at the bottom, looking down at the component side. The dial sits on the right-hand edge, and its three legs are arranged two-and-one:

LegWhat it isShould read
The single leg, pointing up toward the screw terminalThe measuring point — this is the wiper0 to 1.16 V, moves as you turn the dial
Of the two legs pointing down toward the pins: the one nearer the board's edgeThe fixed feed into the dial1.16 V, always
Of the two legs pointing down: the one nearer the chipGround0 V
  1. Unplug that driver's motor at the green screw terminal first. Then nothing can move while you work. Nothing on or around the dial is at a dangerous or damaging voltage, so this part is low-risk.
  2. Apply 24 V — and confirm it is really there. See the warning below; this is the most likely reason for a zero reading.
  3. Black probe on ground. The ground screw terminal on the green block (second from the left, marked with a minus) is the easiest solid contact.
  4. Red probe on the middle leg nearer the board's edge first — the fixed feed. If that reads 1.16 V, everything upstream is working and you can trust what you read on the wiper. If it reads zero, stop: the driver's internal 5 V is not running, which means 24 V is not reaching this board.
  5. Meter on DC volts, 2 V range if it does not auto-range.
The lit LED does not mean 24 V is present

Read from Adafruit's schematic: the three little lights are logic indicators fed from the Arduino's 5 V, not power indicators. The red and green pair show which way the direction wire is set — green for one way, red for the other — and the yellow one flickers when step pulses arrive. All three run whether or not 24 V is connected.

So a light coming on tells you the Arduino is talking to the board. It says nothing about the motor supply. The dial's voltage, by contrast, comes from a 5 V regulator inside the driver chip that only runs when 24 V is on the board's power terminals.

Check 24 V directly at the two left-hand screw terminals on the green block, which is a far easier target than the dial's legs. If that reads 24 V and the dial's fixed leg still reads zero, that driver chip has a problem.

Each of the twelve dials is independent and they will not match out of the box. Setting all twelve is twelve measurements — about fifteen minutes, done once.

Why 1.00 V and not lower

The chip's own datasheet cautions against running the reference below roughly one volt: at low reference voltages, electrical noise and supply ripple become large relative to the reference, and the current regulation gets less precise. Their words: it is "not advised to work with reference voltages below about 0.5V to 1V for full scale," and for best precision a board should be designed so its maximum lands between 2 and 2.4 volts.

Adafruit's board tops out at 1.16 V, so it already sits inside the range the chip maker cautions about. Nothing to be done about that — it is fixed by resistors on the board. But it does mean the sensible move is to use more of the dial's range rather than less. Since imprecise current regulation shows up as chopper noise, and this piece has to be silent, the earlier advice to drop to 0.76 V was pointing the wrong way. Use 1.00 V, and take the heat saving from the standstill setting below instead.

The one free change worth making: standstill power down

Grounding a single pin on each driver cuts the sculpture's resting power to under a third, with no loss of speed, smoothness or precision while moving.

A stepper burns its full set current while standing perfectly still, doing nothing. The TMC2209 has a built-in answer: when it notices the step pulses have stopped, it drops to a little over half current. The datasheet puts the saving at less than 33% of standstill power dissipation.

It is enabled by pulling the chip's combined power-down and serial pin to ground. Adafruit's schematic shows that pin wired only to the header — no pull resistor either way, so on your boards it is floating and the feature is not reliably on. Your wiring manual lists it as unconnected, which matches.

The change: a jumper from pin 9 of the ten-pin header to pin 2 (ground) on each driver. Pin 1 is the end nearest the 5 V logic wire; both are on the same connector you already have wires in, so nothing needs unsoldering.

Twelve motorsStanding still nowStanding still with the pin grounded
dial at 1.00 V (1.3 A)≈ 82 W≈ 27 W
dial fully clockwise (1.53 A)≈ 112 W≈ 37 W
Test this before trusting it

Half current at rest means half the holding torque at rest. If the prisms are well balanced about their shafts, gravity asks almost nothing of the motor and this is free. If any prism is heavy on one side, it could creep off its face while idle — which on this piece would be visible and unacceptable.

So do it on one driver first, leave that prism parked on a face for an hour, and check it has not drifted. Only then do the other eleven. Note also that grounding this pin rules out ever talking to the driver over its serial interface, which this project does not use.

Ignore the formula you will find online

Every guide on the internet assumes a current-sensing resistor of 0.11 ohms, because that is what the cheap 3D-printer driver boards use. Adafruit's board uses 0.05 ohms — components R1 and R2 in their published schematic, "0.05Ω/0.25W". Following the internet's numbers sets roughly 50% more current than intended.

The working figure for this board: current in amps = 1.31 × the voltage you measure, or turned round, volts = 0.76 × the current you want.

The dial cannot reach 2 amps

Adafruit feeds the dial through a fixed divider — 33 kΩ from the chip's 5 V regulator into a 10 kΩ trimmer — so the dial's full travel spans zero to about 1.16 volts, which is about 1.5 amps per phase. Adafruit's "up to 2A max" quotes the peak of the current waveform, about 2.16 amps, which is a different number from the one a motor is rated at.

Sources: Adafruit's published schematic for this breakout (R1, R2 = 0.05 Ω; 33 kΩ into a 10 kΩ trimmer off the chip's 5VOUT pin) and the TMC2209 datasheet current-setting equation.

Why all twelve motors pulse on power-up

Not yet settled. The supply-overload explanation below was built on a current figure the hardware cannot actually reach, so it is weaker than it first appeared.

What is still solid:

  1. The identification sketch on the Arduino sends no step pulses at all until you type a command. Nothing in the code is telling the motors to move.
  2. All twelve pulse together. A wiring mistake affects one motor. Twelve motors moving in unison share only two things — the power rail and the control lines.
  3. The Mean Well LRS-150-24 does protect itself in hiccup mode: on overload it shuts down, waits, and retries. Every retry would jolt all twelve motors at once.

The corrected arithmetic

A microstepping driver holds a constant current vector at standstill, so the two coils share it rather than each carrying the full set current. The heat in one motor is the set peak current squared, times the winding resistance — not twice that.

Overload protection engages at 110–140% of rating, which is 172–218 watts. Even with every dial at maximum the sculpture standing still does not get there. The driver-loss share of those figures is estimated from the chip's on-resistance rather than measured, so treat the range as a range.

So the dials are worth setting deliberately for heat and headroom — but they are probably not the cause of the pulsing on their own. Note too that when all twelve prisms move together, current is back to the full run figure, so the standstill saving does not help during a synchronised move.

The other candidate

If 24 V arrives while the Arduino is unpowered or held in reset, all twelve step and direction lines are floating inputs. Floating inputs pick up noise, and noise on a step line is a step command. That would make all twelve motors twitch at once just as convincingly as a supply hiccup, and it is exactly what the established power-on order guards against: USB first, confirm the serial banner, then 24 V.

What to do, in order

  1. Watch the rail — this one measurement decides it. Put a meter across the 24 V output while it pulses. If the voltage collapses and recovers in time with the pulsing, it is the supply. If the voltage holds steady, the supply is innocent and the cause is on the signal side.
  2. Check the power-on order. Confirm the Arduino is powered and running the sketch — serial banner visible — before 24 V goes on. If the pulsing only happens when 24 V leads, that is the answer.
  3. Prove it in two minutes. Unplug eleven motors at the terminal blocks and leave one connected. Power up. If the pulsing stops, it points at the shared rail rather than the wiring.
  4. Set the current dials deliberately using the voltage table above — 1.00 V on each, giving about 1.3 amps per phase.
  5. Ground the standstill power-down pin on one driver, confirm that prism holds its face for an hour, then do the other eleven. That is the large, free power saving.
  6. Bring them back one at a time. Reconnect motors in small groups, checking after each that the supply stays steady.
  7. Then run the identification sweep to sort out which channel drives which prism.
Your own design notes

The wiring manual states: "The system is calculated to draw ~80W under normal load. The 150W PSU provides ~50% safety headroom." Eighty watts across twelve motors is about 1.5 amps each. That is the top of what the dial can do, and the supply was chosen sensibly for it.

A note on the black-and-red theory

Swapping black and red would not produce this symptom. The two coils are electrically separate, so connecting one wire from each coil to the same bridge leaves that bridge with no circuit at all. A motor wired that way sits dead and silent — it does not buzz. Worth confirming with the meter regardless, but it is not what you are seeing.
